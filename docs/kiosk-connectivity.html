Connectivity Approach — Three Options

SELECTED ✓ Selected approach

Cellular

The kiosk connects to a cell tower via a modem with a SIM card — no connection to the building's network whatsoever. The only Ethernet cable involved is a short internal one between the modem and the kiosk computer, contained entirely within the enclosure. Fully independent from any host site's infrastructure. Works 24/7 regardless of building hours, staff, or network changes.

Viable but dependent

WiFi

The kiosk's computer has a WiFi antenna and connects wirelessly to the building's or site's WiFi network — no cables to the building at all. Clean installation. But it introduces a dependency: the kiosk relies on the host site's network, which may require coordination with their IT team, is subject to their password changes, and can be affected by their outages.

Construction-heavy

Wired Ethernet

A physical cable is trenched or drilled from the building's internal network out to the kiosk — permanent infrastructure work. Reliable and fast, but requires construction coordination, conduit permitting, and ties the kiosk permanently to one site's network. High installation cost per kiosk.

How Cellular Connectivity Works

Technical Overview

The cellular modem is a small device (slightly larger than a deck of cards) with a SIM card slot — conceptually similar to a mobile hotspot. It connects to a cell tower the same way a smartphone does. The kiosk's computer (the brain running the PWA) connects to that modem via an Ethernet cable, exactly the same way it would connect to a regular router. From the computer's perspective, it doesn't know or care whether the connection leads to cellular, WiFi, or wired internet — it just sees "internet access" through that Ethernet port.

Cell Tower → SIM in Modem → Modem (converts to standard internet) → Ethernet cable → Kiosk Computer → PWA in locked browser

Recommendation

Lean: Managed IoT SIM Multi-carrier resilience, month-to-month flexibility, no minimum commitment, and simple dashboards make this the best fit for CSAH's scale. It's not the most familiar option — but it's the most operationally sound for a small nonprofit scaling kiosks incrementally.

Before committing: (1) Confirm Meridian hardware's built-in cellular options — the external modem may not be needed. (2) Get quotes from managed IoT SIM providers (Hologram is a good starting point) and ask about nonprofit pricing. (3) If managed SIM feels like unnecessary complexity, T-Mobile IoT is the fallback: most affordable direct-carrier option with month-to-month availability. (4) Ask AT&T about FirstNet eligibility regardless — CSAH's emergency coordination role may qualify them for public safety network priority. (5) Apply to Twilio.org before signing anything — if approved, Twilio Super SIM could cover connectivity under the same nonprofit credits as SMS and change this recommendation entirely.
SMS provider — not yet decided, but worth exploring before signing a connectivity contract Twilio is the likely pick for the texting feature (2-way reply threading, reliable delivery, works well with lightweight hosting) — not finalized. One detail worth surfacing now: Twilio also sells cellular IoT connectivity directly (Super SIM). If CSAH ends up on Twilio for SMS anyway, using Super SIM for kiosk connectivity instead of a separate carrier consolidates both under one vendor — one dashboard (Twilio Console) showing SMS delivery status, data usage, and SIM health together, one invoice, one support relationship.

The bigger factor: Twilio.org is Twilio's formal nonprofit program, offering credits and discounts to qualifying 501(c)(3) human services organizations — CSAH is a strong candidate. Those credits apply to both SMS messaging and Super SIM connectivity, potentially covering both costs under one program. That would be the strongest nonprofit-fit differentiator in this whole comparison, ahead of any option above. Nothing here changes the Managed IoT SIM recommendation today — but it's worth a Twilio.org inquiry before locking in a connectivity contract, since approval could change the math significantly.

Open Questions

  • Does Meridian's hardware include a built-in cellular modem option? If yes, SIM procurement goes through the hardware vendor or is inserted at deployment — no separate modem purchase.
  • Does CSAH qualify for AT&T FirstNet? Their inclement weather coordination and emergency services partnership role is adjacent to first responder eligibility. Worth a direct inquiry before ruling AT&T out.
  • What is actual data usage per session at the Compass Project kiosks (if analytics are available)? This validates the <200 MB/month estimate and determines whether a per-MB or flat-rate plan is more economical.
  • Will all 5 potential kiosk locations (Union Mission, Goodwill, Bull Street, Greyhound, Memorial) be confirmed before connectivity contracts are signed? Month-to-month flexibility allows adding devices later — but knowing the final count helps with pooled data plan sizing.
  • Get side-by-side quotes from 2–3 managed IoT SIM providers (Hologram is a recommended starting point) before selecting one. Ask each about nonprofit pricing and multi-carrier network coverage in Savannah specifically.
  • Apply to Twilio.org and ask whether nonprofit credits cover Super SIM connectivity alongside SMS. If approved before a connectivity contract is signed, this could change the recommendation above.
